Decoding the Numbers

Lion's Mane Extract Ratios: What 8:1 and 10:1 Actually Mean

A bigger ratio looks like a better product and often is not. The number describes an input, and the thing you care about is the output.

Sources listed at the end Published October 2026 Reading time: 6 min
Finding

The short version

A 10:1 ratio means ten units of starting material were used to produce one unit of finished extract. It describes the input, not the result, so it says nothing by itself about how much of any specific compound ended up in the jar.1 A ratio is only informative when the label also states what the starting material was and reports a beta-glucan figure. Without those two, a higher number is a bigger claim rather than a better product.

  • The ratio describes starting material, not finished content.
  • Ten units of grain-grown mycelium is not ten units of mushroom.
  • A beta-glucan percentage is the number that can be checked.
  • A ratio with no stated starting material means very little.

What the ratio is describing

Read literally, an extract ratio is a statement about a factory, not about a jar.

When a label says 10:1, it is reporting that ten kilograms of starting material were processed to yield one kilogram of finished extract. The intention is to communicate concentration, and in a well-run process it does. The problem is that the figure is entirely dependent on a variable the ratio itself does not mention: what the ten kilograms were.

Ten kilograms of dried fruiting body is a different starting point from ten kilograms of mycelium harvested along with the grain it grew on, and in commercial production the latter is common.1 Both can honestly print 10:1. They are not the same product.

Why a bigger number is not automatically better

Three reasons a higher ratio can mean less than it appears.

  • It tells you nothing about which compounds survived. Concentration by weight is not selectivity. A process can remove a great deal of mass without preferentially keeping the parts anyone is interested in.
  • Starting material quality is invisible in the number. A generous ratio applied to poor or grain-heavy input produces a concentrated version of poor or grain-heavy input.
  • It is not standardized or independently verified. Supplements in the United States are not reviewed for safety and effectiveness before sale the way medicines are, so a ratio on a package is a manufacturer's statement, not a checked figure.23

This is why two products both printing 10:1 can sit at very different prices and both be priced correctly.

The number that can actually be checked

Ask for a beta-glucan percentage. Beta-glucans are the polysaccharides specifically associated with mushrooms, and a stated percentage is a claim about the finished product rather than about the process that made it. It is also testable, which is the important part.

Watch for the substitution: "total polysaccharides" is a much broader category that also captures starch, and starch is what a grain substrate contributes. A grain-grown product can therefore post a high total polysaccharide figure that has little to do with the mushroom. A label that reports beta-glucans has chosen the number that is harder to flatter.

How to read a ratio in practice

  • Ratio plus starting material, or it does not count. "10:1 from dried fruiting body" is a real statement. "10:1 extract" on its own is close to decorative.
  • Prefer a reported beta-glucan percentage over any ratio, and prefer both over either.
  • Compare milligrams per serving, not ratios. A 4:1 extract at a larger serving can deliver more than a 10:1 at a token one.
  • Ask for the certificate of analysis for the batch. A company that tests will send it; one that will not has told you something.
  • Treat a ratio above roughly 20:1 with more skepticism, not less. It is a large claim about a process nobody outside the company has seen.

Where this sits against the evidence

It is worth keeping the whole discussion in proportion. The human research on Lion's Mane is limited, and the most cited recent study is a small pilot trial covering cognitive measures, stress and mood over a short period, which did not establish an optimal extract strength or compare ratios.4 No trial has told us that 10:1 outperforms 8:1 in people, because none has asked. What the research does support is weeks of consistent daily use, which is an argument for buying something you can afford to keep buying rather than the most concentrated product on the shelf.

Before you buy

Talk to a clinician who knows your history before starting a new supplement, especially alongside prescription medication. Persistent difficulty with focus or memory is a reason to have it looked at rather than to keep comparing extract strengths.5

People also ask

What does 10:1 extract mean?

Ten units of starting material were processed to produce one unit of finished extract. It is a statement about the input, so it tells you nothing on its own about how much of any particular compound is in the jar.

Is 10:1 better than 8:1 Lion's Mane?

Not necessarily, and no human trial has compared them. The ratio does not say which compounds survived or what the starting material was, so a higher number applied to grain-heavy input is a concentrated version of grain-heavy input.

What should I look for instead of the ratio?

A stated beta-glucan percentage, because that is a claim about the finished product and it can be tested. Then the milligrams of extract per serving, so you can compare two products on what you actually receive.

Why do some labels only say total polysaccharides?

Because total polysaccharides is a broader category that also captures starch, and starch is what a grain substrate contributes. It is the number that flatters a grain-grown product, which is why a beta-glucan figure is more informative.

Does a high ratio justify a high price?

Only if the label also states the starting material and reports a beta-glucan percentage. Without those, a large ratio is a large claim about a process nobody outside the company has inspected.
