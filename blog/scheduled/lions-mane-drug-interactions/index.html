Safety First

Lion's Mane and Medication Interactions

The honest headline is that formal interaction data is thin. That is a reason to tell your prescriber, not a reason to assume it is fine.

Sources listed at the end Published October 2026 Reading time: 6 min
Finding

The finding

There is no well-characterised list of lion's mane drug interactions, because the human research base is small: a 2025 systematic review covering work from 2000 to mid-2024 found around five randomised controlled trials and three pilot clinical trials in total.1 That is nowhere near enough to map interactions properly. Reported adverse reactions in clinical study include abdominal discomfort, nausea, and skin rash.2 The correct reading of thin evidence is caution and disclosure to your prescriber, not reassurance.

  • No established interaction list exists. The evidence is too small.1
  • Reported reactions: abdominal discomfort, nausea, skin rash.2
  • Absence of documented interactions is not evidence of safety.
  • Tell every prescriber what you take, including supplements.

Why the honest answer is uncertainty

Interaction data comes from large, long, carefully monitored studies. Lion's Mane has not had them.

A 2025 systematic review followed PRISMA, searched PubMed for peer-reviewed work published between January 2000 and June 2024, used the ROBIS tool to assess bias, and registered its protocol on PROSPERO. What it found in humans was five randomised controlled trials, three pilot clinical trials, one cohort study, and one case report, alongside 15 laboratory studies and one computer analysis.1

Interaction signals usually emerge from pharmacology studies and from surveillance across thousands of patients taking many medications. A handful of modest trials cannot produce that. So when a website lists confident lion's mane interactions, ask where the data came from. Often it is reasoning by analogy rather than observation.

What has actually been reported

Memorial Sloan Kettering's herb monograph records adverse reactions reported in clinical study as abdominal discomfort, nausea, and skin rash.2 Those are the documented experiences, and they are mild and uncommon rather than alarming.

Skin rash is worth singling out. Lion's Mane is a mushroom, and allergic reactions to mushrooms are real. Anyone with a known mushroom or mould allergy has a concrete reason to be careful, and that concern does not depend on interaction data at all. Our page on lion's mane side effects covers the reported effects in more detail.

Situations that warrant a conversation

These are not documented interactions. They are circumstances where the stakes of an unknown are high enough that a clinician should be in the loop before you start:

  • You take anticoagulants or antiplatelets. Bleeding risk is the category where unknowns matter most, so flag anything new.
  • You are on diabetes medication. Anything that might touch blood sugar needs to be known to whoever manages your doses.
  • You take immunosuppressants. Including after transplant or for autoimmune conditions, where immune-modulating claims deserve scrutiny.
  • You take psychiatric medication. Mood and cognition are the claimed territory, so your prescriber should know.
  • You are pregnant or breastfeeding. There is no adequate safety evidence, which is itself the answer.
  • You have surgery coming up. Tell the team about every supplement, and ask when to stop.
  • You have a mushroom or mould allergy. A direct allergy concern independent of any drug.

How to handle it sensibly

  • Disclose it like a medication. Supplements belong on the list you give every clinician, including dentists and surgeons.
  • Start one thing at a time. If you add several supplements at once, you cannot attribute a reaction to any of them.
  • Keep the packaging. If something goes wrong, the exact product and dose matter.
  • Stop and seek advice for anything systemic. A rash, swelling, or breathing difficulty needs prompt medical attention rather than watchful waiting.
  • Prefer clearly labelled products. You cannot reason about what you cannot identify. See how to read a lion's mane label.
  • Treat confident online interaction lists carefully. Check whether they cite human data or are extrapolating.

A note on how to read 'no known interactions'

That phrase appears on a lot of packaging and it is doing less work than it appears to. For a substance that has been through large clinical programmes, it means something. For one with a handful of small trials behind it,1 it mostly means nobody has looked hard. The practical difference matters most for people on medication where a small shift would be consequential, which is exactly the group that should be asking a clinician rather than a label.

People also ask

Does lion's mane interact with medications?

There is no well-characterised interaction list, because the human research base is small, amounting to roughly five randomised trials and three pilot trials in a 2025 systematic review. That uncertainty is a reason to tell your prescriber rather than a reason to assume it is fine.

Can I take lion's mane with antidepressants?

Ask the prescriber who manages your medication. Mood and cognition are the areas lion's mane is marketed for, which is exactly why your psychiatrist or GP should know you are taking it, even though no specific interaction has been established.

Is lion's mane safe with blood thinners?

This has not been properly studied, and bleeding risk is a category where unknowns carry real consequences. Raise it with the clinician who manages your anticoagulation before starting anything new.

Who should avoid lion's mane?

People with a known mushroom or mould allergy have a direct reason for caution, since skin rash has been reported. Pregnancy and breastfeeding lack adequate safety evidence. Anyone on medication for a serious condition should check with their clinician first.

Should I stop it before surgery?

Tell your surgical team about every supplement you take and follow their instructions on when to stop. Teams routinely ask people to pause supplements before procedures, and that decision should come from them.
