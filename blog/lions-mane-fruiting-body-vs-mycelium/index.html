Reading the Label

Fruiting Body vs Mycelium: Which Lion's Mane Is Better?

It is the single biggest quality fork in the category. Two products can share a name and a price and still not contain the same thing.

Sources listed at the end Published August 2026 Reading time: 7 min
Finding

The short version

The fruiting body is the actual mushroom. Mycelium is the root-like network the fungus grows first, and in many supplements it is grown on grain and milled together with that grain. For most buyers a concentrated fruiting-body extract is the more reliable choice, because it generally carries higher and more consistent levels of the mushroom's characteristic compounds.12

  • Fruiting body: the mushroom itself, richer in beta-glucans.
  • Mycelium on grain: cheaper, often diluted by leftover starch.
  • Label tell: look for "fruiting body" and a beta-glucan percentage.

What the two parts actually are

A mushroom has a life cycle, and supplements can be made from different stages of it, which is where the confusion starts.

The mycelium is a web of fine threads the fungus sends out to feed, a bit like roots. Under the right conditions the mycelium fruits, producing the visible mushroom, which is called the fruiting body. Both contain compounds of interest, and both are studied.1 The practical difference is how each is grown and sold. Fruiting bodies are typically cultivated to maturity and then extracted. Mycelium for supplements is often grown quickly on a bed of grain such as rice or oats, then the whole mass, mycelium plus grain, is dried and powdered together.

Why mycelium on grain is the sticking point

The issue is not that mycelium is useless. It is that mycelium grown on grain is hard to measure honestly.

  • The grain comes along for the ride. Because the substrate is not separated out, the finished powder is part mushroom material and part leftover starch.
  • Polysaccharide numbers can mislead. A high "total polysaccharides" figure can reflect grain starch rather than mushroom beta-glucans, so the number looks impressive without meaning much.2
  • Concentration tends to be lower. Reviews and product analyses generally find fruiting-body extracts deliver more consistent beta-glucan content than mycelium-on-grain products.1

None of this requires you to distrust every mycelium product. It just means the burden of proof is higher, and most makers who sell mycelium on grain do not clear it.

Why beta-glucans are the better yardstick

Beta-glucans are compounds concentrated in mushroom cell walls, and they have become a common marker for judging extract quality.

The reason a beta-glucan percentage is more useful than a total-polysaccharide number is that beta-glucans are harder to fake with starch. A maker that publishes a beta-glucan figure, verified by a third party, is giving you a number you can actually compare between products.3 A label that lists only total polysaccharides, or no number at all, is leaving you to guess. Note too that the notable human trials of lion's mane, including the 2009 cognitive study and the 2023 pilot, used fruiting-body or whole-mushroom preparations rather than mycelium on grain.45

How to read the label in ten seconds

You do not need a lab to tell these apart. A few words on the panel do most of the work.

  • Green flags. "Fruiting body" or "fruit body," a stated beta-glucan percentage, an extraction ratio, and a current third-party certificate of analysis.
  • Yellow flags. Vague phrases like "full spectrum," "mushroom complex," or "mycelial biomass" with no substrate disclosure.
  • Red flags. Only "total polysaccharides" listed, no mention of the plant part, and no testing you can verify.

People also ask

Is fruiting body or mycelium lion's mane better?

For most buyers, a concentrated fruiting-body extract is the more reliable choice. The fruiting body is the actual mushroom and generally carries higher, more consistent levels of the characteristic compounds. Mycelium grown on grain is cheaper to produce but is often sold mixed with its grain substrate, which can dilute the active content.

What is mycelium on grain?

Mycelium is the root-like network the fungus grows before it forms a mushroom. In many supplements it is grown on a grain such as rice or oats, then dried and milled together with that grain. The finished powder is a blend of mycelium and leftover starch, which is why its polysaccharide numbers can look high without reflecting mushroom beta-glucans.

Why do beta-glucans matter in lion's mane?

Beta-glucans are a class of compounds concentrated in the mushroom's cell walls and are often used as a marker of extract quality. A label that lists a beta-glucan percentage is more informative than one that lists only total polysaccharides, because total polysaccharides can include starch from a grain substrate rather than mushroom content.

How can I tell if a lion's mane is fruiting body?

Read the label for the words fruiting body or fruit body. Be cautious of vague terms like full spectrum or mushroom mycelial biomass, which often signal a mycelium-on-grain product. A beta-glucan percentage and a current third-party certificate of analysis are strong signs the maker is confident in what is inside.

Does mycelium have any benefits?

Mycelium contains its own compounds and is an area of active research, so it is not worthless. The issue is mostly transparency and concentration: mycelium grown on grain and sold without separating the substrate makes it hard to know how much mushroom you are actually getting. A clearly labeled fruiting-body extract removes that guesswork.
